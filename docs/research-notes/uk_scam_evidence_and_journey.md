## Q1. Which scam types most affect UK older adults, by number of victims vs money lost? Are older adults over-represented in incidence or in losses?

### Takeaway
Two kinds of data point in opposite directions. In the population survey (CSEW, England & Wales), the 75+ group is *less* likely than average to be a fraud victim (6.0% vs 7.8%), and most fraud is low-value card or account fraud that gets refunded. But in police report data for the high-harm, phone-originated, in-person scams (courier fraud, bank or police impersonation), victims are heavily concentrated among the oldest: about 62% of courier-fraud reports come from people aged 76–96, with average losses above £15k. So older adults are over-represented in *severity* within specific contact-based scam types, not in overall incidence.

### Inferences
- By **number of victims**, the dominant fraud affecting 65+ is bank and credit account fraud: card not present and unauthorised transactions. It is mostly no-contact and mostly refunded (85% fully reimbursed, all ages), so it is common but rarely life-changing. A product aimed at this is aimed at a problem banks already absorb.
- By **money lost per victim and harm**, the consequential types for the oldest group are the phone-originated, contact-based ones: courier fraud and bank or police "safe account" impersonation (average £15k–20k, concentrated in 76–96), plus remote-access fraud. Investment and romance fraud carry the biggest totals, but their victims peak around 50–74, not 75+.
- "Older adults" splits into at least two target groups with different scam types:
  - 55–74, online and investment-active: investment and romance scams, phishing clicks highest in 65–74.
  - 75+, landline and in-person: courier, impersonation, doorstep and postal scams.
- A hackathon product should pick one group, not both.
- Telecoms-originated APP fraud is 17% of cases but 28% of losses. The phone channel is where the high-value APP fraud is.

### Gaps
- No UK source found that breaks APP *losses* by victim age (UK Finance and PSR publish none; the PSR dashboard has no age field in what was seen).
- 2025 absolute figures for bank or police impersonation (cases and £) are in the UK Finance PDF, which couldn't be text-extracted.
- No current (2024–26) national Action Fraud figures for remote access or tech-support scams by age.
- The CSEW fraud-by-age figures are prevalence of being a victim, not money lost by age. ONS publishes no loss-by-age table.

## Q2. Which factors predict victimisation better than age alone?

### Takeaway
UK evidence on individual risk factors is thin and mostly descriptive. The strongest UK signals are disability (CSEW: 10.4% vs 7.1%) and, for the high-harm doorstep, postal and phone scams, living alone and recent bereavement (Trading Standards: 62% of new scam victims live alone, 33% recently bereaved). Cognitive decline has the best longitudinal evidence, but it is US-only. Higher income and education *raise* CSEW fraud prevalence, which probably reflects more exposure (more accounts and online activity) rather than vulnerability.

### Cited Findings
- **Disability (UK, survey):**
  - CSEW YE Mar 2026: disabled 10.4% fraud prevalence vs 7.1% not disabled; long-term sick 9.9%. — [ONS YE Mar 2026 Table 1](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/datasets/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwalesappendixtables/yearendingmarch2026/fraudandcomputermisuseyemar26.xlsx)
  - In the YE Mar 2025 adjusted model, disability is associated with higher odds. — [ONS YE Mar 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2025)
- **Living alone and bereavement (UK, Trading Standards data cited by Age UK):** 85% of new scam victims are 65+; of those, **62% live alone and 33% had a recent bereavement.** The average postal-scam victim is 75. 85% of doorstep-crime victims are 65+. The date is not given (Age UK press release Feb 2018, footnote 4). — [Age UK, 2018](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2018/february18/age-uk--action-fraud-to-combat-scams-targeting-older-londoners/)
- **Marital status (CSEW YE Mar 2026):** widowed adults have *lower* fraud prevalence (6.1%) than average (7.8%). This conflicts with the bereavement signal above. The likely reason is unit and type: the CSEW counts any fraud, mostly card fraud, while Trading Standards counts doorstep, phone and postal scam victims. — [ONS Table 1](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/datasets/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwalesappendixtables/yearendingmarch2026/fraudandcomputermisuseyemar26.xlsx)
- **Income, education, exposure (CSEW YE Mar 2026):** household income £52k+ 9.5% vs under £10,400 6.4%; degree 9.1% vs no qualifications 4.7%. Going to a pub weekly or more is linked to 10.5% vs 6.6% for none, which is an exposure and activity marker. — [ONS Tables 1–2](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/datasets/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwalesappendixtables/yearendingmarch2026/fraudandcomputermisuseyemar26.xlsx)
- **Receiving care at home (CSEW, phishing):** people receiving care were slightly *less* likely to receive a phishing message (40.6% vs 46.8%) and to click (2.3% vs 2.6%). — [ONS Table 9](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/datasets/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwalesappendixtables/yearendingmarch2026/fraudandcomputermisuseyemar26.xlsx)
- **Age UK claim (advocacy, no data shown):** older people who live alone or have cognitive impairment are at greater risk of certain scams. — [Age UK, Apr 2024](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2024/millions-of-over-50s-fear-opening-their-doors-and-answering-their-phones-because-of-scams-says-age-uk)
- **FCA vulnerability framing (UK):** 49% of UK adults (26.4m) showed at least one characteristic of vulnerability in May 2024. The FCA groups these into four drivers: poor health, negative life events, low resilience and low capability. — [FCA Financial Lives 2024](https://www.fca.org.uk/financial-lives/financial-lives-2024)
- **PSR:** 14% of APP reimbursement claims (about £7m) came from consumers flagged as vulnerable (Oct–Dec 2024, UK Faster Payments). — [PSR "The story so far", 15 May 2025](https://www.psr.org.uk/news-and-updates/thought-pieces/thought-pieces/the-story-so-far-a-snapshot-of-what-we-ve-seen-since-our-app-scams-reimbursement-requirement-went-live/)
- **Cognition (US, longitudinal):** Rush Memory and Aging Project, 935 dementia-free older adults followed for about 6 years. Lower baseline scam awareness was associated with later Alzheimer's dementia and mild cognitive impairment, and with Alzheimer's pathology at autopsy (Boyle et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2019). The study shows association, not cause. — [Rush University](https://www.rushu.rush.edu/news/scam-susceptibility-may-early-sign-dementia)
- **Cognition (Canada):** An Ontario study of 151 community-dwelling older adults found poorer cognitive skills, not social factors, distinguished victims from non-victims. — [Toronto Metropolitan University](https://rshare.library.torontomu.ca/articles/journal_contribution/The_Role_of_Cognition_Personality_and_Trust_in_Fraud_Victimization_in_Older_Adults/21440829) (snippet)
- **Loneliness and precarity (US, mixed evidence):** prospective US data associate older age, loneliness, financial or emotional precarity and risky financial preferences with victimisation. A systematic review says there is no consensus on how cognition and physical or mental conditions contribute. — [Kessler Scholars summary](https://kesslerscholars.org/publications/contextual-and-social-predictors-of-scam-susceptibility-and-fraud-victimization); [PMC9484557](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9484557/) (snippets)
- **Confidence vs checking (UK, UK Finance survey, secondary, about 2023):** 18–24s were most confident at spotting scams, but only 27% always check. Over 60% of over-55s say they always check unexpected requests. — [LBC](https://lbc.co.uk/business/f45eece849f34f27ad85d5a9cb8c3716)

### Inferences
- For a product, the strongest UK-evidenced segment for *consequential* scams is: **aged 75+, living alone, recently bereaved or newly isolated, with a landline, and possibly early cognitive decline**. This is the profile in Trading Standards and courier-fraud data. It is *not* the profile with the highest CSEW fraud prevalence, which peaks at 45–64 and among affluent, digitally active, disabled people.
- "Low digital confidence" may be partly *protective* against cyber-enabled fraud (75+ cyber fraud 2.9% vs 4.6% for all adults). But it pushes risk toward phone and in-person channels, where losses per victim are highest.
- Disability is the one UK survey-validated risk marker that holds across age groups.

### Gaps
- No UK longitudinal (ELSA-based) study of fraud victimisation risk factors was found. The search didn't surface one; it may exist but wasn't located.
- The Springer UK papers ("Gullibility, loneliness, exploitation or just bad luck?", 2026; "Fear of fraud amongst older adults", 2025; "Does age matter? seniors' romance fraud", 2025) were blocked.
- No UK data was found on what share of high-loss victims have a cognitive impairment diagnosis.

## Q3. What does the scam journey look like for impersonation/APP, courier and remote-access scams, and where does the victim act?

### Takeaway
All three journeys start with an unsolicited **phone call** (or a pop-up that prompts a call) and work by keeping the victim on the line or under instruction. A fake authority creates urgency ("your account is compromised" or "help catch a corrupt bank worker") and isolates the victim from verification, including by **coaching them to lie to genuine bank staff**. The point of loss differs by type:
- Impersonation and APP: the victim makes a bank transfer to a "safe account".
- Courier: cash, cards, currency, gold or jewellery are handed to a courier at the door.
- Remote access: the victim installs remote software and then logs in, transfers or shares OTPs.

### Cited Findings
**Courier fraud** (Which?, 28 May 2024, read directly):
1. Cold call.
2. Caller poses as police or bank; story of fraudulent card use or an "arrest" needing the victim's help.
3. **Call-back trick**: the victim is told to ring the number on the card, but the scammer holds the line open, so the victim reconnects to the scammer.
4. Victim told to withdraw cash, exchange currency, buy high-value items, or hand over their card; a courier collects it.
- Source: [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/courier-scams-fraudsters-stole-over-28.7m-last-year-ajDnq2L4vpqM)
- 2025 variant: victims are sent to several jewellers to buy gold or jewellery for collection. — [City of London Police, Jun 2026](https://www.cityoflondon.police.uk/news/city-of-london/news/2026/june/over-70s-targeted-as-courier-fraud-exceeds-21-million-in-2025-with-london-and-home-counties-hit-hardest/) (secondary)

**Bank or police "safe account" impersonation (APP):**
- Fraudster claims to be the bank or police and urges the victim to move money to a "safe" account. — [UK Finance press release 2026](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/news-and-insight/press-release/fraud-report-2026-press-release); [digit.fyi on 2024 data](https://www.digit.fyi/?p=126399)
- **Coaching:** criminals tell victims their own bank is involved in fraud, then coach them to lie if a genuine bank worker asks why they are moving money. Lloyds reports average impersonation loss £3,516 (+10% year on year). Both points come from a search summary; the Lloyds pages returned errors. — [Lloyds, Aug 2026 (PDF)](https://www.lloydsbank.com/assets/media_centre/pdf/lloyds-impersonation-scams-august-2026.pdf); [Lloyds news page](https://www.lloydsbank.com/media-centre/2026/seven-words-reveal-youre-talking-to-a-scammer.html)
- Santander: the fraudster tells the customer to lie about the payment purpose so the bank cannot show a relevant warning. — [Santander press release](https://www.santander.co.uk/about-santander/media-centre/press-releases/santanders-head-of-fraud-targeted-as-impersonation) (snippet)
- Financial Ombudsman case study: the caller coached the victim through the whole £9,225 transfer and told her to ignore on-screen warnings and move through them quickly. — [FOS case study](https://www.financial-ombudsman.org.uk/decisions-case-studies/case-studies/helped-complaint-safe-account-scam) (snippet)

**Remote access / computer software service fraud** (dated):
- Starts with a call from someone using a well-known company name (e.g. Microsoft), claiming a computer problem and asking for remote access. Some victims instead see a **pop-up** prompting them to call. — [Action Fraud](https://www.actionfraud.police.uk/news/victims-still-being-targeted-by-computer-software-service-fraud) (snippet)
- AnyDesk named as a tool. A detective says older people are targeted because they are "vulnerable and accessible", often at home. — [Portsmouth News](https://www.portsmouth.co.uk/news/crime/warn-your-relatives-a-senior-detective-speaks-out-as-it-supporta-scammers-fleece-hampshire-victims-out-of-thousands-1014343) (snippet)
- One-time passcodes compromised to register digital wallets or make transactions. This is a general mechanism, not specific to older adults. — [UK Finance 2026](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/news-and-insight/press-release/fraud-report-2026-press-release)

**Contact channel (CSEW):** where the offender contacted the victim, telephone was the first contact in 39% of incidents (all ages, YE Mar 2025). — [ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2025)

**Journey summary (each stage is drawn from the sources above):**

| Stage | Safe-account APP | Courier | Remote access |
|---|---|---|---|
| First contact | Call or text "from bank/police" | Cold call to landline | Call "from Microsoft/BT" or pop-up |
| Credibility | Spoofed number, knows details, call-back trick | Call-back trick, "police operation" | "Your PC is infected/hacked" |
| Urgency / secrecy | "Account compromised", "bank staff involved" | "Help catch corrupt bank staff, tell no one" | "Fix now or lose data or money" |
| Action / payment | Bank transfer, often coached past warnings | Withdraw cash, buy gold, hand card to courier | Install AnyDesk, log in to bank, share OTP |
| Recognition | Later contact with the real bank or family | Often after courier leaves or relative notices | Unexplained transfers |
| Recovery | PSR mandatory reimbursement (FPS, up to £85k) | Cash handover is not a payment, so no PSR reimbursement scheme applies (inference) | Depends on whether payments were authorised (APP) or unauthorised |

### Inferences
- The decisive act happens *during an ongoing call*, often with the scammer still on the line or calling back. An intervention has to work in real time and outside the channel the scammer controls (not "call the number on your card" from the same landline).
- Because victims are coached to lie, bank-side questioning that relies on what the customer says is structurally weak. Coaching is most explicit in safe-account and courier scams.
- Courier fraud ends in a physical handover, so it bypasses APP reimbursement. Losses are high (about £15k–20k average) and fall on the oldest. This is the most consequential and least protected niche found.

### Gaps
- No UK data was found quantifying the **share of APP or impersonation victims who were coached** to lie.
- No UK data was found on the time from first contact to payment, or how many payments happen in one session versus several days. That would size the intervention window.
- No current UK source was found with a detailed remote-access journey or loss figures for 2024–26.

## Q4. What evidence exists that a pause, trusted-person call, bank intervention or third-party check prevents loss?

### Takeaway
The best UK outcome evidence is for **in-branch intervention (Banking Protocol)**, about £61m prevented in 2024, and for **call blockers for older people** (more than 99% of scam and nuisance calls blocked in a 2020 pilot whose users averaged 75). Reimbursement rules are linked to fewer APP scams (about 35,000 fewer, £73m a year less lost). There is **no published outcome evidence** found for the 4-day payment delay, and only self-reported claims for Confirmation of Payee. No controlled UK evidence was found on "call a trusted person" interventions, though it is standard official advice.

### Cited Findings
- **Banking Protocol** (branch staff spot suspected scam, police attend):
  - 2024: £61.3m prevented (2023: £54.7m); 12,034 emergency calls; 136 arrests. These figures are from UK Finance Annual Fraud Report 2025 (May 2025), seen via a search summary only. — [UK Finance Annual Fraud Report 2025 PDF](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/system/files/2025-05/UK%20Finance%20Annual%20Fraud%20report%202025.pdf)
  - Cumulative: over £400m prevented since launch, per a 2025 speech. — [David Postings speech, Economic Crime Congress 2025](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/news-and-insight/speeches/speech-david-postings-economic-crime-congress-2025)
  - Customers helped are typically over 65 (UK Finance-cited police data, 2020). — [Take Five](https://www.takefive-stopfraud.org.uk/news/bank-branch-staff-and-police-team-up-to-stop-19-million-of-fraud-in-first-half-of-2020/)
  - 2025 national figure not found. An unverified blog figure of £59m was not used.
- **Call blockers (National Trading Standards Scams Team, 2020 pilot):**
  - More than 1,000 units installed; 283,700 calls received, of which 35% were scam or nuisance; 98,701 blocked (more than 99% of scam and nuisance calls).
  - **Users averaged 75 years old**, and received about 23 scam or nuisance calls a month, against an estimated 7 for the general population.
  - Projected saving about £20.2m. One analysis estimates £36 saved per £1 spent. The savings are modelled projections.
  - 3 months after install: 92% received no more scam or nuisance calls; 95% no longer felt threatened.
  - — [NTS news](https://www.nationaltradingstandards.uk/news/scam-and-nuisance-phone-calls-return-to-precovid19-levels); [NTS call blocking PDF](https://www.nationaltradingstandards.uk/site_assets/files/National%20Trading%20Standards%20Scams%20Team%20-%20Call%20Blocking.pdf); [NTS free call blockers](https://www.nationaltradingstandards.uk/news/free-call-blockers-for-victims-of-scam-and-nuisance-phone-calls) (secondary summaries)
  - Earlier project (2017–18): 108,918 calls blocked; worry about unwanted calls fell from 79% to 17%. — [NTS](https://www.nationaltradingstandards.uk/news/successful-project-blocks-over-100000-nuisance-calls)
- **PSR mandatory reimbursement (from 7 Oct 2024):**
  - Frontier Economics evaluation (PSR, 1 Jul 2026): APP losses about £73m a year lower; nearly 35,000 fewer APP scams; in-scope claims reimbursed at 97%; all claims at 65% (up from 54%). The PSR notes variation between firms. — [PSR](https://www.psr.org.uk/news-and-updates/latest-news/news/payment-fraud-falls-by-73m-following-psr-reimbursement-scheme/)
  - First 3 months: 86% of claims passed from sending to receiving firm within 2 business hours; 2% rejected under the consumer standard of caution; the claim excess cannot be applied to vulnerable consumers. — [PSR, May 2025](https://www.psr.org.uk/news-and-updates/thought-pieces/thought-pieces/the-story-so-far-a-snapshot-of-what-we-ve-seen-since-our-app-scams-reimbursement-requirement-went-live/)
  - Q1 2026 dashboard: about 438,300 claims over 18 months; 88% (£316m) of in-scope losses reimbursed (secondary). — [PSR dashboard](https://www.psr.org.uk/information-for-consumers/app-scams-reimbursement-dashboard/)
- **Payment delay ("friction"):** since 9 Oct 2024 (FCA guidance says 30 Oct 2024), banks may hold outbound payments for up to 4 business days on reasonable suspicion of APP fraud. The FCA says 4 days is a maximum, not a default. **No outcome data was found.** — [FCA GC24/5](https://fca.org.uk/publication/guidance-consultation/gc24-5.pdf); [Grant Thornton](https://www.grantthornton.co.uk/insights/final-guidance-on-delayed-payments-to-prevent-app-fraud/)
- **Confirmation of Payee:** the only effectiveness claim found is the operator's self-report of a "significant impact". No independent evaluation was found. — (search result; no primary evaluation located)
- **159 hotline (Stop Scams UK):** about 1 million calls since its 2021 launch (reported April 2025). It covers banks with more than 99% of UK retail current accounts. No evidence on losses prevented was found. — [MoneySavingExpert](https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/news/2025/04/call-bank-number-martin-lewis/); [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/uk/news/breaking-news/uk-fraud-losses-hit-1-28-billion-as-scam-calls-rise-587641.aspx)
- **Official "pause and verify" advice:**
  - Hang up and wait at least 15 minutes, or use a different phone, before calling back on a trusted number. Call 159. Banks and police never ask you to buy valuables or hand over money. — [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/courier-scams-fraudsters-stole-over-28.7m-last-year-ajDnq2L4vpqM)
  - Being told to lie to your bank is itself a sign of a scam. — [North Yorkshire Police via Planet Radio](https://planetradio.co.uk/greatest-hits/harrogate-yorkshire-dales/news/fraudsters-impersonating-police-officers-in-north-yorkshire/) (snippet)
- **Coaching defeats bank warnings:** see Q3 (Lloyds, Santander, FOS). Victims are told to ignore warnings and give false payment purposes, which stops banks showing a relevant warning.

### Inferences
- The interventions with UK outcome evidence share a pattern: they put a **third party outside the scammer's script** in the loop (branch staff, police, a call-blocker's whitelist), or they remove the contact channel altogether. The interventions that depend on the victim answering honestly (warnings, payment-purpose questions) are the ones coaching defeats.
- The Banking Protocol only works where the victim goes into a branch. Branch closures and online or app banking shrink that window. A consumer-side "trusted person check" is the analogue for remote payments and doorstep handovers. That is a plausible gap, but it has no UK outcome evidence.
- Call blockers have the strongest evidence for the 75+ landline segment, so any new product competes with or complements an existing, cheap, proven tool.

### Gaps
- No UK RCT or quasi-experiment on "call a trusted person before paying" interventions was found.
- No outcome data was found for the 4-day payment delay or for Confirmation of Payee on impersonation scams.
- The 2025 Banking Protocol national figure was not verified.
- The practitioner "what works" paper (ScienceDirect, 2026) was blocked.

## Q5. Is the primary unmet need detection, advice, reassurance, trusted-person verification, reporting, or recovery? What do victims say they needed?

### Takeaway
The evidence points to **fear and loss of confidence** as a widespread harm even among non-victims, and to **health deterioration after victimisation** as the consequential harm. Recovery is now largely covered for APP transfers (PSR reimbursement) and unauthorised card fraud, but not for cash or courier handovers. No UK study was found that asks victims directly what intervention would have stopped them. That is a key gap and a reason to do user research before building.

### Cited Findings
- **Fear among older people (Age UK, 16 Apr 2024; over-50s; method not given):**
  - 4.9m fear answering the phone; 2.8m fear opening the door.
  - 2.1m say fear stops them using smartphones; 1.8m say it stops them using the internet.
  - An average of 4 people aged 50+ are scammed every minute in England & Wales (CSEW-derived).
  - Age UK's 2022–23 Scams Prevention Programme: 25,850 people at awareness talks; 4,979 had one-to-one support.
  - — [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2024/millions-of-over-50s-fear-opening-their-doors-and-answering-their-phones-because-of-scams-says-age-uk)
- **Health impact (Independent Age, 4 Oct 2024; over-65s; method not stated on the page):** nearly 3 in 5 over-65s targeted; average loss £4,000; 31% of victims reported long-term mental health problems; 12% negative physical health effects. — [Independent Age, Hidden cost of scams](https://www.independentage.org/policy-and-research/research-reports/hidden-cost-of-scams)
- **Qualitative UK study:** Fenge & Lee (2018, *British Journal of Social Work*), 12 older victims and carers; covers health and wellbeing impact and reluctance to disclose. — [Bournemouth eprints](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/31171/) (snippet)
- **Advocacy claim (no data shown):** some victims lose independence or move into residential care after a scam. — [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2024/millions-of-over-50s-fear-opening-their-doors-and-answering-their-phones-because-of-scams-says-age-uk)
- **CSEW emotional impact (all ages, YE Mar 2025):** 71% of fraud victims reported an emotional impact; 23% loss of confidence or feeling vulnerable; 13% shame, embarrassment or self-blame. — [ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2025)
- **Recovery for APP transfers:** 61% of APP losses reimbursed in 2025 on UK Finance's wider scope; 88–97% of in-scope PSR claims. — [UK Finance](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/news-and-insight/press-release/fraud-report-2026-press-release); [PSR](https://www.psr.org.uk/news-and-updates/latest-news/news/payment-fraud-falls-by-73m-following-psr-reimbursement-scheme/)

### Inferences
- **Detection** of generic phishing is crowded: telcos, banks and email filters already do it, and 75+ adults rarely click (1.7%). **Recovery** for bank transfers is now largely regulatory. Neither looks like the primary unmet need.
- The most defensible unmet need is **real-time, trusted-person verification during a live high-pressure contact**, for isolated people aged 75+ facing phone or doorstep impersonation and courier scams. It should work even when the victim has been told to keep it secret. A secondary need is **reassurance and confidence restoration**, since fear itself is causing withdrawal from phones, the internet and the front door.
- This is inference from the evidence, not something victims were recorded asking for.

### Gaps
- No UK survey was found asking older victims what would have stopped them, or what support they wanted afterwards. Independent Age's full report PDF was not read and may contain this.
- No data was found on how often family members detect scams in progress.

## Q6. Under-reporting: what share is reported, and why?

### Takeaway
Only about **1 in 9 fraud incidents** (10.7%, CSEW YE Mar 2026, all ages) reach the police or Action Fraud/Report Fraud. The main reason is that victims reported to their **bank instead** (52%). Shame is a minor stated reason in the CSEW (about 1%). By contrast, Age UK's 2017 poll of people aged 65+ found 22% of those targeted told *no one* because of embarrassment. The two measure different things (victims vs those targeted; reasons for not reporting to police vs telling anyone), so they should not be merged.

### Cited Findings
- **Reporting rate (CSEW, E&W, incidents, all ages), % of fraud incidents reported to police or Action Fraud:**
  - YE Mar 2026: **10.7%**; YE Mar 2025: 13.4%.
  - By type, YE Mar 2026: bank and credit 8.6%; consumer and retail 9.9%; all other fraud 22.8%.
  - — [ONS annual supplementary tables YE Mar 2026, Table A6](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/datasets/crimeinenglandandwalesannualsupplementarytables/march2026/annualsupplementarytablesyemar26.xlsx)
  - Assumption: the final data column is taken to be Apr 2025–Mar 2026. The table's title says it runs to YE March 2026, but the column header was truncated in extraction.
- **Reasons for not reporting fraud (CSEW YE Mar 2026, incidents not reported to police or Report Fraud):**
  - Reported to bank or building society: 52.1% (up significantly from 47.6%).
  - Thought another authority would report it: 18.0%.
  - Too trivial: 15.6%.
  - Private matter: 7.2%.
  - Didn't know where to report: 1.8%.
  - **Too ashamed or embarrassed: 1.1%.**
  - Didn't see self as a victim: 1.2%.
  - — [ONS nature of fraud tables YE Mar 2026, Table 5](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/datasets/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwalesappendixtables/yearendingmarch2026/fraudandcomputermisuseyemar26.xlsx)
  - Note that "Action Fraud" is now "Report Fraud".
- **Age UK poll (Kantar, Jun/Jul 2017, 1,367 people aged 65+, GB), among those who believed they had been targeted:**
  - About two-fifths (about 4.9m) did not report to an official channel.
  - 36% told only friends or family.
  - **22% told no one because they felt too embarrassed.**
  - — [Age UK, Feb 2018](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2018/february18/age-uk--action-fraud-to-combat-scams-targeting-older-londoners/)
- Age Scotland: 70% of targeted older people in Scotland did not report; a quarter told no one because of embarrassment. — [Sunday Post](https://www.sundaypost.com/fp/scammers-targeted-400000-elderly-people-in-scotland) (snippet)
- Older ONS figure: 13% of fraud incidents came to police or Action Fraud attention (CSEW YE Jun 2018). — [ONS evidence to Treasury Committee, Dec 2018](https://uksa.statisticsauthority.gov.uk/submission/office-for-national-statistics-written-evidence-to-the-treasury-committees-inquiry-on-economic-crime)
- ONS warns that recorded fraud (Action Fraud 299,046 offences in YE Mar 2025) cannot be combined with survey counts to produce a reporting rate. — [ONS YE Mar 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/crimeandjustice/articles/natureoffraudandcomputermisuseinenglandandwales/yearendingmarch2025)
- Police say remote-access victim numbers are thought to be much higher than reports, partly because of embarrassment. — [Action Fraud](https://www.actionfraud.police.uk/news/victims-still-being-targeted-by-computer-software-service-fraud) (snippet); [Measham PC / Leicestershire Police](https://www.meashamparishcouncil.gov.uk/news/2018/03/message-from-leicestershire-police1) (snippet)

### Inferences
- In the CSEW, under-reporting to police is mostly a *channel* effect: people go to the bank, not the police. Shame matters more for high-harm, contact-based scams among older people, where telling *anyone*, including family, is the barrier. That supports a product hypothesis around low-shame, pre-agreed trusted-person disclosure. It is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- Police report data (courier, romance, remote access) therefore undercounts, and probably undercounts more among the most ashamed and isolated, so the true concentration among 75+ in high-harm scams may be higher than the report data shows.

### Gaps
- No age-specific CSEW reporting rate or reasons-for-not-reporting table was found. The ONS tables are for all ages.
- No post-2017 Age UK reporting poll was found. The widely quoted "only 5% of fraud is reported" figure could not be traced to a primary source, so it should not be used.
