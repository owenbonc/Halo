# Trusted contact / caregiver alert model for protecting UK older adults from scams (hypothesis to validate)

All URLs accessed 2026-10-23. "Fetched" = page content read directly. "Search summary" = figure seen in search-result excerpts only, full source not opened; treat as lower confidence and verify before quoting. Non-UK evidence is labelled by jurisdiction. Vendor or bank press material is labelled as such.

Overall framing for the report writer: desk research shows (a) trusted-person mechanisms exist in UK banking and US brokerage, (b) uptake is modest where measured, (c) family members are the leading known perpetrators of financial abuse of older people in UK helpline data, and (d) privacy and control drive acceptance of monitoring tech. Desk research does NOT establish whether UK older adults want a trusted person alerted about scam messages, who they would pick, whether that person can respond in time, or what pause/revoke terms they would expect. Those need primary interviews.

## 1. What do UK older adults say about involving family/friends in scam checks or financial oversight?

### Takeaway
I found no UK survey that directly asks older adults whether they would want a family member or friend alerted about suspected scam messages or payments. Adjacent UK evidence: people prefer limited, specific help over handing over control. Informal sharing of bank access is common and risky. Many over-75s have no power of attorney.

### Cited Findings
- Money and Mental Health's report "If I Needed Someone" (October 2025) surveyed its Research Community (people with lived experience of mental health problems or caring). The most popular option was "specific, one-off support", followed by "ongoing but still limited support". A smaller group wanted to give a trusted person "greater control". The article gives no percentages; the chart values were not readable. The sample is people with mental health experience, not older adults specifically. Fetched. — [Money and Mental Health, "How to improve third party access in financial services", 16 Oct 2025](https://www.moneyandmentalhealth.org/how-to-improve-third-party-access-in-financial-services/); full report: [If I Needed Someone (PDF)](https://www.moneyandmentalhealth.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/If-I-Needed-Someone.pdf)
- The same organisation recommends "a minimum suite of third party access tools" across the sector (examples given: carers' cards, account notifications, third-party payment controls). It also calls for expanding the UK Finance industry working group via the FCA and for an Office of the Public Guardian (OPG) review of Power of Attorney. Fetched. — [Money and Mental Health](https://www.moneyandmentalhealth.org/how-to-improve-third-party-access-in-financial-services/)
- In a 2019 Co-op survey of over-45s, 25% said they had access to a relative's bank account other than a spouse's, mostly to help manage money. This is a commercial survey and is not specific to older adults. Search summary. — [Co-op press release](https://www.co-operative.coop/media/news-releases/uk-adults-risk-it-as-relatives-manage-their-money-for-them)
- FCA-commissioned qualitative research on older consumers' coping mechanisms found that older people and carers find retail banking hard to use. An LPA gives peace of mind, but limited awareness of and confusion about informal third-party access reduce its effectiveness. Search summary. — [FCA, Coping mechanisms / third party access research (PDF)](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research/coping-mechanisms-third-party-access.pdf)
- A 2023 Just Group study reported that 3.4m over-75s had not arranged a power of attorney. The main reason given was not feeling at the right stage of life. Insurer research reported by trade press. Search summary. — [International Adviser](https://international-adviser.com/3-4m-over-75s-yet-to-arrange-a-power-of-attorney)
- UK qualitative research using 80 written accounts from older adults in the Mass Observation Archive found that scams affect health and wellbeing even without financial loss, and that victims face scam-related stigma. This is relevant to whether people would tell a family contact. Search summary; the full text returned 403. — [Journal of Adult Protection, "Older adults and 'scams': evidence from the Mass Observation Archive" (2021)](https://emeraldinsight.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JAP-07-2020-0030/full/html)
- In a Hourglass/YouGov survey (year not stated; published for Safer Ageing Week 2024), 16% in England and Scotland did not believe scams targeting older people count as abuse. 26% in England and Wales did not recognise using a Power of Attorney for personal financial gain as abuse. Fetched. — [Hourglass, Economic Abuse by Numbers](https://wearehourglass.org/economic-abuse-numbers)

### Inferences
- The preference for "specific, one-off" or "limited" help suggests a scoped, opt-in, per-event alert may be more acceptable than ongoing visibility. That is only a hypothesis; the population was not older adults.
- Stigma and embarrassment may make some older people unwilling to have a family member told about a near-miss. This is a key design risk to test.

### Gaps
- No UK survey data found on: willingness to share scam alerts with a trusted person, preferred type of contact (child, friend, bank, charity), or expected terms (opt-in, pause, revoke, what the contact sees). Needs primary research.
- Not checked: the FCA Financial Lives survey tables on third-party access by age. Over-75 figures were not found in search; the report writer should not cite any.

## 2. Existing trusted-contact mechanisms and their uptake/evidence

### Takeaway
UK mechanisms fall on a spectrum. At one end is full legal authority (LPA, registered with the OPG). Next come formal bank mandates and "trusted person" cards. Lightest is opt-in payment confirmation by a contact (Monzo Trusted Contacts). I found no published UK uptake or effectiveness data for the bank-level features. In the US, the FINRA trusted contact rule has about 38–40% uptake, and FINRA proposed changes in 2026 to raise it.

### Cited Findings
UK
- **Lasting Power of Attorney.** The OPG annual report 2024–25 covers 1 Apr 2024–31 Mar 2025 (primary PDF). — [OPG Annual Report 2024 to 2025](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6878c0f77ea20916863638b1/opg-annual-report-2024-to-2025.pdf)
  - Commentary on the report says the register holds more than 9 million LPAs and EPAs, up about 1.3m in a year. A critic notes the register is not automatically updated when donors or attorneys die. Search summary. — [Law Gazette](https://www.lawgazette.co.uk/practice-points/how-to-enhance-the-opgs-safeguarding-role/5124392.article); [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/number-of-lpas-registered-soars-by-28)
  - In the year to March 2024 the OPG received 1.37m applications to register powers of attorney, up from 1.07m. Search summary. — [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/number-of-lpas-registered-soars-by-28)
- **Bank third-party mandates.** Nationwide's Third Party Mandate lets a trusted person act in branch only, for a chosen duration of up to 12 months in total. Bank help page; search summary. — [Nationwide, Guide to Third Party Mandates](https://www.nationwide.co.uk/help/supporting-someone-with-their-finances/third-party-mandate)
- **Trusted Person Card.** Bank of Scotland (Lloyds Banking Group) offers a debit card linked to the customer's own current account, with a different card number and PIN, for a trusted person to use. Bank help page; search summary. — [Bank of Scotland, Customer access tool](https://www.bankofscotland.co.uk/helpcentre/someone-to-manage-your-affairs/customer-access-tool.html)
- **Age UK's view.** Carers' cards with controls on how they can be used are often much safer than giving someone your own debit card. Search summary. — [Age UK, access to cash during and after lockdown](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/discover/2021/march/access-to-cash-during-and-after-lockdown/)
- **Monzo Trusted Contacts** (launched July 2024; trade press reporting a bank announcement, so effectively vendor messaging). It is opt-in. A customer nominates a friend or family member who also banks with Monzo. Transfers or savings withdrawals over a set limit are held until that contact approves them. It launched with "Known Locations" and "Secret QR Codes". No uptake or outcome data found. — [Fintech Futures](https://www.fintechfutures.com/digital-banking/monzo-announces-new-feature-to-help-protect-customers-against-impersonation-scams); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/neobank-monzo-new-anti-fraud-features); [Payment Expert](https://paymentexpert.com/2024/07/01/monzo-uk-fraud-prevention-tools/)
- **Nationwide Call Checker** (Jan 2026; bank press release, so vendor messaging). This is not a trusted-contact feature. It lets customers check in the app whether a caller really is Nationwide. Nationwide says impersonation scams are 17% of its reported fraud cases, and customers over 65 make up 55% of those cases. Which? reports that Barclays, Monzo, Revolut and Starling offer similar caller-check tools. — [Nationwide press release, 12 Jan 2026](https://www.nationwide.co.uk/media/news/nationwide-launches-new-call-checker-as-brits-lose-confidence-due-to-scam-calls); [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/new-nationwide-call-checker-tool-how-is-your-bank-fighting-fraud-aMLGG7M1ASS7)
- **Industry work.** A UK Finance blog says eight major banks and building societies agreed a Vulnerability Taskforce principle: "Easy for friends and family to support". It describes a planned industry framework for third-party mandates. The blog is undated and the current status is unverified. — [UK Finance, "A little help from friends and family"](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/blogs/little-help-friends-and-family)
- **Building society model.** A BSA-linked guide describes three-party arrangements between customer, supporter and society. Examples range from a letter of authority for a single withdrawal to information-sharing with a trusted supporter. Search summary. — [Market Harborough BS, "Need support with your finances"](https://mhbs.co.uk/document/need-support-with-your-finances/)

US (FINRA; label US)
- FINRA Rule 4512 requires broker-dealers to make reasonable efforts to get a trusted contact's name and details. Naming a trusted contact does NOT give that person authority over the account. Search summary. — [FINRA, Trusted Contact Persons (2022 exam report)](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/guidance/reports/2022-finras-examination-and-risk-monitoring-program/trusted-contact-persons); [Dechert on FINRA FAQs (2018)](https://www.dechert.com/knowledge/onpoint/2018/1/finra-issues-faqs-on-rules-relating-to-financial-exploitation-of.html)
- Uptake:
  - A FINRA Investor Education Foundation survey (year unclear) found only 38% of respondents had named a trusted contact; a FINRA executive called this too low. Search summary. — [ThinkAdvisor, June 2023](https://thinkadvisor.com/2023/06/28/heres-what-a-trusted-contact-is-and-isnt-finra-exec)
  - Sommer & Lim, using the 2021 US National Financial Capability Study (n=2,046), found about 40% had named a trusted contact. Search summary. — [Journal of Financial Services Marketing 2024, via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jofsma/v29y2024i2d10.1057_s41264-023-00227-7.html)
- FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-02 (2026) proposed amendments to raise adoption. A CFP Board/FPA/NAPFA comment letter says many customers still have not named a contact. A NASAA comment letter says FINRA's 2025 and 2026 oversight reports found some firms not giving written disclosure of when a trusted contact may be called. A rule filing, SR-FINRA-2026-018, followed in Aug 2026; contents not verified. Search summary. — [NASAA comment letter](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/NoticeComment/NASAA%20Comment%20Letter%20re%20FINRA%20Reg%20Notice%2026-02%20%2803-09-2026%29.pdf); [CFP Board et al. letter](https://www.cfp.net/-/media/2026/miscellaneous/cfp-board-fpa-and-napfa-comment-letter-in-response-to-finra-2602-senior-exploitation-final.pdf); [SR-FINRA-2026-018](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2026-08/SR-FINRA-2026-018.pdf)

South Korea (label KR)
- Korean authorities and five major banks prepared an opt-in service. When an account holder aged 65+ withdraws above a set amount, a pre-designated family member is notified; the target was launch by year-end. This is a plan; launch status unverified. Search summary. — [Herald Corp](https://biz.heraldcorp.com/article/10883558)

### Inferences
- The FINRA model is the closest analogue to "alert a trusted person". About 60% of US investors still have not named one after years of a mandatory "reasonable efforts" duty, so uptake is a real risk even when the option is offered at onboarding.
- UK bank tools range from authority to act (mandate, card) to approval gates (Monzo). An alert-only, information-sharing model that gives no power to act sits at the light end. It also limits what a contact can do once alerted (see section 5).

### Gaps
- No uptake, usage or effectiveness data found for Monzo Trusted Contacts, Lloyds/Bank of Scotland Trusted Person Card, or Nationwide mandates.
- Lloyds, Starling and Nationwide "trusted contact"-style alert features were not confirmed to exist.
- Australian models (e.g., bank trusted-contact or ABA industry guidelines) were not researched within budget.
- I did not open OPG guidance on attorneys' duties.

## 3. Prevalence of financial abuse of older people by family/carers in the UK, and what it means for family visibility

### Takeaway
The national prevalence survey is old: 2007, with 0.6% of over-66s financially abused by family, friends or care workers in the past year. Hourglass helpline data shows sons and daughters are the largest known perpetrator groups for abuse overall. Giving a family member visibility of an older person's messages could hand information to the very person who abuses them. The design must handle this, not ignore it.

### Cited Findings
- UK Study of Abuse and Neglect of Older People (O'Keeffe et al., 2007; Comic Relief and Department of Health), covering people aged 66+ in the community:
  - 2.6% reported mistreatment by family, close friends or care workers in the past year; 4.0% when neighbours and acquaintances are included.
  - Financial abuse was 0.6%, second to neglect (1.1%).
  - Care homes were excluded.
  - Search summary of the LSHTM record. — [LSHTM Research Online](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/29615); [UK Data Service record via B2FIND](https://b2find.eudat.eu/dataset/28e3d3ab-1cae-519b-b503-122e56bdb8a4)
- A review on MaPS's evidence hub estimated that 1.2% of older people had recently been financially abused by someone they knew. Search summary. — [MaPS Evidence Hub, "The extent and nature of financial abuse among older people"](https://evidence-hub.maps.org.uk/en/research-library/review/the-extent-and-nature-of-financial-abuse-among-older-people)
- Hourglass helpline data for 2022–23 (callers, not a representative sample; all abuse types, not only financial):
  - 22.3% of known perpetrators were sons and 19.4% were daughters.
  - 16.5% were intimate partners.
  - Search summary. — [Hourglass, Understanding the perpetrators of abuse (PDF)](https://wearehourglass.org/sites/default/files/inline-files/Hourglass-Understanding-the-perpetrators-of-abuse.pdf)
- Hourglass's 2020 "Growing Old in the UK" survey: 22% had experienced abuse as an older person or knew someone who had. Hourglass extrapolates this to about 2.7m affected (all abuse types). This is a "know someone" measure, not true prevalence. Search summary. — [Domestic Abuse Commissioner / Hourglass conference slides (PDF)](https://domesticabusecommissioner.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/C4-Hourglass-DAC-Conference-March-2023.pdf)
- Hourglass says most financial abuse it sees is by family members or trusted friends, including misuse of LPAs. Financial-abuse calls rose from 1,676 (2021) to 2,909 (2022). Search summary of Hourglass and press. — [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/financial-abuse-how-to-spot-the-signs-and-where-to-turn-for-support-a1WS04m95Kz7); [WealthBriefing](https://www.wealthbriefing.com/html/article.php?id=198342)
- Hourglass also reports (fetched):
  - Over £53m in reported financial losses from older helpline callers over three years.
  - Only 14% of economic abuse cases report a financial loss.
  - Abuse of older people costs the UK over £16bn a year.
  - [Hourglass, Economic Abuse by Numbers](https://wearehourglass.org/economic-abuse-numbers)
- OPG:
  - Investigations rose to 3,647 in 2023–24 from 2,849.
  - In 2024–25, 73% of investigations needed no further action (77% the year before), and 24% led to Court of Protection action (15% the year before).
  - Search summary. — [Mental Capacity Report, Sept 2025 (PDF)](https://www.mentalcapacitylawandpolicy.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Mental-Capacity-Report-September-2025-Property-and-Affairs.pdf)
  - The OPG handled more than 11,000 concerns in a year, only about a third within its legal powers. An online form for reporting misuse of an LPA/EPA or deputyship has existed since October 2025. Search summary of OPG blog. — [OPG blog, "Investigations at OPG", 16 Jan 2026](https://publicguardian.blog.gov.uk/2026/01/16/investigations-at-opg)
- US regulator view (label US): a large share of elder financial abuse is by family members, and it is significantly under-reported. — [OCC speech 2015 (PDF)](https://www.occ.treas.gov/news-issuances/speeches/2015/pub-speech-2015-45.pdf)

### Inferences
- Message visibility is riskier than payment alerts. A family member who can see an older person's messages gains insight into finances, relationships and vulnerabilities. That could be used for coercive control or economic abuse, which Hourglass data puts mostly with sons and daughters.
- Safeguards to test in interviews, not assume:
  - The older person chooses the contact, with no default family member.
  - The contact sees a minimal alert ("a possible scam was flagged") rather than message content.
  - The older person can pause or revoke without the contact being told.
  - A non-family option exists (bank, charity, helpline).
  - Coercion indicators lead to a route that bypasses the contact.

### Gaps
- No up-to-date (post-2007) representative UK prevalence figure for financial abuse of older people. A new UK prevalence study was not found in this search.
- No breakdown of perpetrators specific to financial abuse, from a representative sample, was found.
- Safeguarding Adults Board / NHS Digital "Safeguarding Adults Collection" figures on financial abuse by age and source of risk were not retrieved within budget. They are a recommended follow-up source.

## 4. Autonomy, dignity, paternalism and surveillance in monitoring tech; alert fatigue and relationship conflict

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed reviews consistently find privacy and control to be the main barriers to older adults accepting monitoring. Family and staff usually drive adoption, and their priorities differ from those of the older person. I found no published evidence on alert fatigue or family conflict in scam or caregiver-alert products.

### Cited Findings
- **Video-based assistive living tech** (scoping review; international, mostly non-UK). Privacy worries outweigh other factors, tied to feeling watched and fear of data misuse; safety and independence are the main draws. Search summary. — [JMIR / PMC10186188](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10186188/)
- **Community-dwelling older adults** (scoping review of 52 studies). Acceptance remains low, often because tech does not match real needs. Only 8 of 52 studies involved older adults in study design. Search summary. — [PMC11745446](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11745446/)
- **Low-income independent living** (Ageing & Society; label US setting). A passive monitoring system's assumptions disrupted residents' behavioural autonomy. Residents' priorities (privacy, control) differed from those of staff and family, who usually drive adoption. Search summary. — [Ageing & Society, "Active subjects of passive monitoring"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/ageing-and-society/article/abs/active-subjects-of-passive-monitoring-responses-to-a-passive-monitoring-system-in-lowincome-independent-living/76B9ABCC3CF4D71D88117FA4E3860EAC)
- **Passive in-home monitoring** (Ageing & Society scoping review, 34 studies). Privacy is often missing from technology-acceptance frameworks, and individualist privacy models may miss how older adults experience privacy. Search summary. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/165F654FB5275B0DD075FD0A4C174A03/core-reader)
- **Aged care** (ARIIA evidence summary; label Australia). Some residents felt restricted by constant surveillance while others valued safety. It flags the risk of people being pressured into technology and the value of being able to refuse. Search summary. — [ARIIA, Technology ethics (PDF)](https://www.ariia.org.au/sites/default/files/2023-02/ARIIA-technology-ethics-and-technology.pdf)
- **Smart homes** (review). Ethical issues group into privacy, social support and autonomy; adoption is likelier when specific criteria are met. Search summary. — [CORE, "Ethics and Acceptance of Smart Homes for Older Adults"](https://core.ac.uk/works/8828567)

### Inferences
- A trusted-contact alert is a form of monitoring. Products built for the family buyer risk the same mismatch documented in passive-monitoring studies. The older person should be the account owner and controller.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or industry evidence found on alert fatigue among contacts receiving scam or financial alerts, or on false-positive tolerance.
- No evidence found on relationship conflict caused by caregiver financial-alert products (e.g., US vendors such as EverSafe or Carefull were not researched; any such material would be vendor marketing).

## 5. Availability of contacts in real time, and fallback channels (verified)

### Takeaway
I found no data on how often a nominated relative is reachable at the moment a scam is unfolding; this must be explored in interviews. Official fallback channels exist and were verified as below. Action Fraud has been replaced by Report Fraud.

- A design can route alerts to an always-available official channel (159, the bank) as well as, or instead of, a relative. This removes dependence on a family member answering the phone. ### Gaps
- No data on reachability of adult children or relatives in real time (e.g., time to respond to a call or text). Needs primary research or a diary study.
- Not verified: whether the Citizens Advice consumer helpline handles scam reports (it historically passed reports to Trading Standards, but not confirmed here); whether the Age UK Advice Line handles scam queries; the Report Fraud number from reportfraud.police.uk itself.

## 6. People with no suitable trusted contact

### Takeaway
A material minority of older people are lonely or have no children. A model that depends on a family contact will exclude them unless there is a non-family fallback. Good UK figures on "no one I could ask" were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Age UK's analysis of Understanding Society estimates 940,000 people aged 65+ in the UK (7%) are often lonely. Nine in 10 of those who are often lonely are also unhappy or depressed. Search summary. — [Understanding Society, Age UK loneliness impact](https://www.understandingsociety.ac.uk/impact/age-uk-loneliness/)
- Age UK's ELSA analysis: over-50s who are widowed are over five times more likely to be often lonely than those in a relationship. Search summary. — [Age UK, All the Lonely People (2018)](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/latest-press/articles/2018/october/all-the-lonely-people-report/)
- Ageing Without Children (campaign group) says its 2015 survey found that people ageing without children most feared having no one to speak up for them. It cites estimates of about 4m people over 50 without children. These are advocacy figures, not official statistics. Search summary. — [AWOC](https://awwoc.org/post/ageing-without-children-what-we-ve-learned-in-our-first-3-years); [TLAP/AWOC slides (PDF)](https://www.thinklocalactpersonal.org.uk/_assets/Introduction-AWOC-TLAP-event-.pdf)

### Inferences
- People who are isolated may be the most exposed to scams and the least able to nominate a contact. The model needs a non-family default (bank via 159, charity helpline, or an in-product "check with a person" service) to avoid excluding them.

### Gaps
- No official (ONS) figure retrieved for the share of over-65s with no living children or no close confidant.
- No data linking "no trusted contact" to scam victimisation.

## 7. Interview method guidance

### Takeaway
GOV.UK gives baseline consent rules: an information sheet, ongoing right to withdraw, data deleted on withdrawal, and adapted formats. Trauma research with older adults suggests that asking about victimisation can have a favourable benefit-to-cost ratio when there are screening and support procedures. I found no Age UK or Independent Age published research-methods guide specific to scam interviews.

### Cited Findings
- **GOV.UK Service Manual** on consent (fetched) — [GOV.UK, Getting users' consent for research](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/getting-users-consent-for-research)
  - Get a record of informed consent covering who runs the research, its purpose, the data collected, use and sharing, and retention.
  - Give the information sheet beforehand in an accessible format (print, email, read aloud).
  - Participation is voluntary and withdrawal is possible at any time. Remind participants at the start and end and whenever they seem uncomfortable or confused. Stop and delete data if they withdraw.
  - For vulnerable adults (unable to care for themselves or at risk of harm or exploitation), get consent from both the participant and a responsible adult.
  - For visual or cognitive impairments, read forms aloud. For motor impairments, record verbal consent.
- **Research with older adults on trauma** (label US). Gagnon & DePrince (Univ. of Denver, 2015) surveyed 99 adults aged 60+. They judged the benefits of taking part in trauma research to outweigh the costs. PTSD and depressive symptoms were linked to stronger emotional reactions. The authors conclude it can be done "within the context of mindful interview and screening procedures". Search summary. — [Digital Commons @ DU](https://digitalcommons.du.edu/gssw_facultyscholarship/72)
- **Trauma-informed protocol example** (label Iran; minors, not older adults). Participants set the pace and could pause, reschedule or stop; support contacts were provided; each session had a debrief. Search summary. — [arXiv 2601.16321](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.16321)

### Inferences (neutral interview plan guidance, derived from the above; not prescribed by any source)
- Do not ask for amounts lost, bank names, account details or scam transcripts. Ask about decisions and feelings ("Who, if anyone, would you want to hear from at that moment?") rather than incident forensics.
- Use neutral, non-leading framing. Present the trusted-contact idea as one of several options, alongside "bank only", "helpline only" and "nobody", so the hypothesis is not confirmed by design. Ask about the downsides explicitly (privacy, burden on the contact, family tension).
- Screen out people in current crisis. Offer pause and stop at any time. Hand out a fallback sheet with the verified numbers in section 5. Debrief at the end.
- Interview potential contacts separately from older adults, so that older people can speak freely about not wanting family involved, and so coercion is not masked.
- Use only third-person probes to explore abuse risk ("Some people worry a relative might misuse this…"). Do not ask participants to disclose abuse. Have a safeguarding escalation route ready (Hourglass helpline; number not verified here).
